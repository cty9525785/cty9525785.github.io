Design and modeling of a programmable morphing structure with variable stiffness capability

Tianyu Chen, Xudong Yang, Yifan Wang*

School of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering, Nanyang Technological University, Singapore

*Corresponding author: yifan.wang@ntu.edu.sg

International Journal of Mechanical Sciences 284, 109699 (2024)

Summary

Robots and deployable structures often need to change shape and then hold that shape under load. Adding a separate stiffening mechanism to a morphing structure makes it bulky, harder to control, and can stop it from reaching its target shape. This paper studies a scaled structure that does both with one mechanism, and turns it into a design method: rules for choosing its geometry, finite element simulations of what sets its shape and stiffness, and three devices built with it.

1 Working principle #

The structure has two layers with triangular teeth: a base layer, and a row of scales hinged to it. Both sit in a thin latex bag. With no pressure applied, the layers slide freely and the strip is soft enough to lie flat or sag.

Pumping the air out of the bag presses the scales onto the base layer. The scales' teeth are spaced wider than the base layer's, so they mesh only when the strip bends: it curls until every tooth sits flush, which happens at the curvature the teeth were cut for. Pressed together, the two layers then work as one thicker plate, so the strip is also stiff.

2 Design method #

The design method, which the paper calls layered tessellation, starts from the target shape. The curve is drawn with the structure's thickness and filled with alternating triangles. The triangles are split into two layers, each layer is straightened by turning its triangles, a thin layer is added to its back to join them, and the upper layer is cut into scales.

Four independent parameters define a structure: the target radius r, the thickness h, the number of scales ns and the number of periods on each scale np. From the geometry of one period, the paper derives the conditions under which the scales still mesh with the base layer when the strip lies flat.

Those conditions bound the design. With the smaller triangles as the base layer and one period per scale, the thickest possible structure grows with the target radius and falls quickly as scales are added, and more periods per scale narrow the range further. Using the larger triangles as the base layer triples it, up to 120 mm thick at a 60 mm radius, but it too shrinks quickly once a scale carries more than one period.

3 Finite element model #

Two-dimensional plane strain models in Abaqus/Explicit include the scales, the base layer, the latex bag and the test fixtures. The printed TPU is modeled as elastic with a measured modulus of 55.7 MPa, the latex as a neo-Hookean solid, and the friction between each pair of materials comes from inclined plane tests.

The models match three-point bending tests at negative pressures from 0 to 90 kPa, and they track how the curvature approaches its target as the pressure rises. The simulated final curvature is slightly higher than the measured one, which the paper attributes to the PTFE tape on the printed parts.

4 Effects of friction, scales and tooth shape #

Friction between the scales and the base layer decides whether the structure can morph at all. A force analysis of one unit, with each period treated as a rigid body hinged at its vertices, gives the friction coefficient above which a scale can no longer slide on the base layer: the layers lock before the strip reaches its shape.

Simulations of whole strips show the same kind of threshold. Their stiffness rises with friction, but above a critical coefficient, about 0.2 with four scales and 0.6 with eight, they no longer reach their target and may even bend the wrong way.

More periods per scale raise the stiffness but lower the curvature the strip reaches, a trade-off between morphing and stiffness.

Changing the shape of the teeth overcomes the trade-off: with superellipse teeth of exponent 1.9, the structure reaches the highest stiffness and the highest curvature of the designs studied.

5 Demonstrations #

Printed from PLA for higher stiffness, the structure becomes a soft gripper, a phone stand and the hinges of a foldable box, all driven by a negative pressure of 90 kPa.

Each finger of the gripper is a strip designed for a quarter circle of radius 63.66 mm, 2 mm thick. Soft, the gripper folds flat; under pressure it opens out within 0.3 s, and it lifts a 147 g load, 7.35 times its own weight, not counting its frame.

The phone stand is one strip of eight scales with two periods each, 4 mm thick and 3 cm wide. It takes its shape in about 11 s and then holds a phone.

In the box, the scaled structures are only the hinges, each designed as a quarter circle, between flat faces 2 mm thick and 3 cm wide. The box folds within one second, holds 400 g, and lifts 20 g.
